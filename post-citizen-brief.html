I Built My Way Back Into the News

Paola Garcia Alzate · April 27, 2026 · 4 min read
```

My daughter was six months old when the war with Iran started. I remember being completely out of the loop — not because I didn't care, but because I was in that new-mom fog where every hour is its own emergency and the outside world just stops existing for a while.

That hit me hard. I'm a policy analyst. Staying informed isn't just a habit — it's part of how I think, how I work, how I understand the world. And suddenly I had no time for it. No time to read three different outlets. No time to figure out who was saying what. No time to even know what I was missing.

"How can I gather the important news — from both the left and the right — without spending an hour I don't have?"

That question sat with me during night feedings. And somewhere around 2am, instead of doom-scrolling, I started describing what I wanted to an AI.

What I actually built

I had never built a website before. No coding background. No technical team. Just a very specific problem and a lot of stolen minutes during nap time.

I described what I wanted: a news briefing that would read itself to me, with two anchors presenting different perspectives — one from the left, one from the right — so I could get the full picture without having to seek it out myself. I wanted opinion cards. I wanted a ticker at the bottom like a real broadcast. I wanted it to feel like turning on the news, not reading a summary.

Three days later, The Citizen Brief existed. Animated anchors. Live tickers. Opinion cards from the Washington Post and the Wall Street Journal sitting side by side. AI reading the news out loud while I fed my daughter at 3am.

The moment I realized something had shifted

It wasn't when the code first ran. It was when I opened it on my phone in the dark, one hand holding my daughter, and heard the anchors read the news to me — and I actually felt caught up. That was the moment.

I had built a tool that solved my exact problem. Not a workaround. Not a compromise. The actual thing I needed.

What this changed for me

I've always been a policy person — someone who analyzes systems, identifies gaps, proposes solutions. What I didn't realize until now is that those same instincts apply to building software. You identify a problem. You think about what the ideal solution looks like. You work backwards from the outcome.

The only thing that changed is that now I can actually build the solution, not just describe it in a memo.

That's what vibe coding gave me. Not the ability to write code — I still can't do that. The ability to build things that work, for real people, starting with myself.

My daughter is still young. I'm still in the nap-time windows. But The Citizen Brief runs every day. And I'm already building the next thing.

```
